# Novelty map

## Conclusion

This project is **not** the first demonstration that PISA performance declines
with later test position. That fact is well established and OECD's PISA 2025
Volume I already reports randomized first- versus second-hour comparisons for
science, mathematics, and reading. The viable contribution is narrower:

1. the first audit of the newly released **LDW** scale under the randomized
   hour/order design;
2. mirrored estimates showing that order changes the student's measured
   **relative strength across LDW and a conventional domain**;
3. LDW-specific scored-response and process-summary evidence; and
4. cross-system heterogeneity and learner-type interactions using the new 2025
   questionnaire environment.

## Nearest-neighbour literature

| Study | Data and design | Main estimand/result | Overlap with this project | Remaining margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OECD (2026), *PISA 2025 Results, Volume I*, Annex A1 | PISA 2025; random booklet allocation; domain in hour 1 versus hour 2 | Conventional-domain hour effects and domain-order patterns; performance is often lower in hour 2 | Same cycle and assignment logic | Does not report LDW as the focal outcome, LDW process summaries, or the mirrored LDW–domain comparative-advantage estimand |
| OECD (2023), *PISA 2022 Results, Volume I*, “How much effort…” | PISA 2022; first/second hour and rotated linear-mathematics item sets | Reading about −14 points, science −8, mathematics −4 on average across OECD countries; distinguishes position and order | Same high-level estimand in earlier core domains | New cycle and LDW task architecture; no LDW comparative-advantage analysis |
| Borgonovi and Biecek (2016) | PISA 2012 rotating booklets | Country-level “academic endurance” from early-versus-late performance | Establishes PISA rotation as an endurance design | LDW-specific block assignment, system-level survey/PV inference, and measurement of relative domain strength |
| Wu, Debeer, Buchholz, Hartig, and Janssen (2019) | PISA 2006 science, 2009 reading, 2012 mathematics; multilevel IRT | Item-cluster position effects and interactions with gender, SES, language, interest, effort, perseverance | Closest predecessor to our heterogeneity section | Different domains/cycles and estimand; this package studies randomized full-hour LDW placement and directly released plausible values/process summaries |
| Brown, Kaur, Kingdon, and Schofield (2025) | Randomized training plus PISA/TIMSS block-order evidence | Treats cognitive endurance as malleable human capital with educational gradients | Gives the strongest economic interpretation of persistence/endurance | Our paper is about assessment measurement and cross-domain ranking, not the production function of endurance |
| Borgonovi and Andrieu (2025) | 153,603 students in 27 PISA 2018 systems; item-position GLMM | Internet use, baseline accuracy, and persistence in digital tasks | Very close to digital-use heterogeneity and position-related performance decline | Not PISA 2025 LDW; does not exploit the LDW mirrored form pairs or its new process-summary variables |
| Mehou and Leventhal (2026) | PISA 2022 US science; explanatory IRTree | Item position and cognitive demand predict rapid-guessing/disengagement; interactive items reduce disengagement | Closest process/engagement predecessor | Single-country science item model rather than international LDW hour assignment and released LDW score effects |
| Debeer and Janssen (2013) | PISA 2006 illustration; IRT position model | Models item difficulty as position-dependent and permits individual differences | Foundational psychometric framework | Our package uses transparent randomized form contrasts, survey weights, plausible values, and meta-analysis rather than a new IRT model |

## Contribution that survives the audit

The strongest framing is:

> Randomized test order changes the measured comparative advantage of students
> between an autonomous digital-learning task and conventional academic domains.
> LDW's interactive environment does not eliminate position sensitivity; its
> score loss is accompanied by less time and fewer actions and by more responses
> coded as disengaged.

This is stronger and more defensible than any of the following claims:

- “the first causal estimate of PISA test fatigue”;
- “the first evidence of item-position effects in PISA”;
- “LDW directly measures autonomous learning free of test context”; or
- “the process fields reveal complete self-regulation strategies.”

## What would materially strengthen novelty

1. Full LDW event logs linking tutorial, worked-example, feedback, and strategy
   sequences to randomized hour assignment.
2. A formal correction showing how order-sensitive LDW measurement changes
   system rankings, SES gaps, or classification of “hidden learners.”
3. A psychometric model jointly estimating LDW ability and position-sensitive
   engagement, validated against the simple plausible-value ITT.
4. External outcomes showing whether LDW-first or order-adjusted scores better
   predict later learning or educational attainment.

Until one of these becomes feasible, novelty rests on the new domain and the
comparative-advantage interpretation rather than on a new test-position method.
